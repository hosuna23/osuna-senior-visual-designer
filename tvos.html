FitNudge for the big screen.

Designed from the couch.

People already work out in the living room, squinting at a phone propped on the coffee table. This concept brings FitNudge to Apple TV: a focus-driven interface you navigate with a remote, readable from ten feet away, with your iPhone and Apple Watch along for the ride.

Why the living room

The workout already happens in front of the TV.

In my FitNudge research, 75% of participants trained three to six days a week and most already used a wearable or a fitness app. For anyone following along at home, the phone is the weak link: too small to follow form cues from a mat, and buried under notifications. The TV is already in the room, already on the wall, and already the biggest screen people own.

Problem

Small screens, big rooms

Form cues, timers and reps are hard to read from a mat six to ten feet away on a phone.

Goal

Start in one click

From the home screen to a running workout in one selection, with today’s plan already waiting.

Goal

Glanceable mid-workout

Time, move and heart rate readable at a glance, without a single extra button press.

Design principles for this concept
  • Content is the interface. Art and type carry the screen; chrome steps back.
  • Focus is the cursor. Every state must be obvious from across the room.
  • Fewer, bigger decisions. Each click costs more with a remote, so every screen offers one clear next step.
  • The phone is a partner. Heart rate, typing and sharing move to the device in your hand.
How I would measure it
Time from launch to started workoutTarget under 10 s
Focus errors per sessionTarget under 1
Week-4 return rate on TV vs. phoneTarget higher

Targets for a future usability study. This is a concept; no results are claimed.

Designing for distance

Big type, safe edges, obvious focus.

I studied Apple’s Human Interface Guidelines for tvOS before drawing anything, then set the concept’s own rules. Turn on the overlay to see the title-safe area on the live prototype.

Legibility at ten feet

Body text never goes below 29 pt on a 1920 × 1080 canvas, and titles sit at 57 pt and up. Light text on dark art, with a scrim wherever imagery runs behind type.

Safe areas

Primary content stays 60 pt from the top and bottom and 80 pt from the sides, so nothing is cropped by overscan.

Focus is the cursor

There is no pointer, so the focused item scales, lifts and catches light. Rows keep their horizontal position when you move up and down.

One remote, few buttons

Swipe or arrow to move, click to select, Back to go up a level. Text entry and heart rate move to iPhone and Apple Watch.

End to end

From the couch to cooldown.

The core journey, step by step. Each step names the device doing the work, because the best multi-device experiences hand off quietly.

Hardware as part of the design

Remote clickpad for navigation, Watch for biometrics, iPhone for text and sharing. Each device does what it is best at.

Shared screen, personal data

Profiles switch from the top shelf. Heart rate and history stay with the person, not the TV.

Siri as a shortcut

“Start my workout” skips straight to the timer. Voice is an accelerator, never the only path.

Built in, not bolted on

Accessible from the first sketch.

  • VoiceOver reads each card as title, length and progress, in visual order.
  • Reduce Motion swaps parallax and scale for a brightness change and outline.
  • Bold Text and Increase Contrast keep timers legible on bright art.
  • Captions on by default for coaching, with audio descriptions of each move.
  • Low-impact alternatives on every move, one click away.
Intelligence in the product

Adaptive, but explainable

Up Next reorders based on what you skipped and how recent sessions felt. Each suggestion says why (“Lighter day after two strength sessions”) so it never feels random.

AI in my process

More directions, faster

I used AI tools to generate early layout and naming variations, draft VoiceOver labels and pressure-test edge cases. Every final decision, visual and line of copy was mine.

Validation plan

What I would test next.

This is a concept, so the honest next step is to put it in front of people on a real TV. Here is the plan I would run.

1

Couch test

Five participants, a TV at 10 feet, and the prototype driven by a Bluetooth remote.

2

Observe

Time to start, wrong turns, and whether people can read cues from a mat.

3

Iterate

Tune focus scale, type sizes and row order from what we see.

4

Build natively

Rebuild key screens in SwiftUI previews to test real focus behavior.

I haven’t shipped a living-room product yet, so I designed one to learn the platform’s rules. I’d love to do it for real.
